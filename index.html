The problem

Most cells in your body are replaced on a schedule. Your gut lining turns over in days and your red blood cells in months. Most of your neurons were made before you were born, and they have to keep working for eight or nine decades.

  • Name the three things every disease profile in this field contains.
  • Explain why the field moved so slowly for decades, and what has changed since 2020.

The adult human brain holds roughly 86 billion neurons. It is about 2% of body mass and uses about 20% of the body's energy at rest. Very few of those neurons can be replaced. Adult neurogenesis exists in a few niches in rodents, and how much happens in the adult human hippocampus is still argued about. In practice, a neuron that dies is gone for good.

A neurodegenerative disease is one in which specific groups of neurons lose function and then die, progressively, over years. Strokes and head injuries kill neurons too, but they do it in one event. Neurodegeneration keeps going on its own after it starts.

How big the problem is

The World Health Organization estimates that about 57 million people lived with dementia in 2021, and Alzheimer's disease accounts for perhaps 60 to 70% of cases. More than 10 million people have Parkinson's disease, and its numbers are growing faster than population ageing alone would predict. ALS, frontotemporal dementia and Huntington's disease are rarer, but they strike younger people and kill faster. Age is the largest single risk factor for almost all of them.

The three parts of every disease profile

Experts describe each disease with the same three components. Once you notice them, papers become much easier to place.

  1. A protein that misbehaves. Usually it misfolds and accumulates as a deposit that a pathologist can stain. Tau, amyloid-β, α-synuclein, TDP-43 and huntingtin are the main ones.
  2. A set of cells that dies first. Each disease has a signature: dopamine neurons in the substantia nigra in Parkinson's, motor neurons in ALS, medium spiny neurons of the striatum in Huntington's. This is called selective vulnerability, and it is one of the field's biggest open questions.
  3. A clinical syndrome. Symptoms follow from which circuits fail. Memory loss when the hippocampus goes, slowness of movement when striatal dopamine runs out, weakness when motor neurons die.

Why progress was slow

  • The disease is silent for a long time. In Alzheimer's, amyloid starts building up 15 to 25 years before memory problems. By the time someone sees a neurologist, much of the damage is done.
  • You cannot easily sample the organ. Brain biopsy is almost never done. For a century, diagnosis was confirmed only at autopsy.
  • Animals don't age like people. Mice live about two years and do not naturally get these diseases. Researchers force them with mutant human genes, and many drugs that worked in those mice failed in people.
  • Patients are mixed. Two people with the same diagnosis can have different biology underneath, and most older brains contain several pathologies at once.
  • Trials are slow and noisy. Decline is measured with rating scales over 18 months or more, so each trial costs years and hundreds of millions of dollars.

What changed recently

  • Blood tests. Plasma p-tau217 now detects Alzheimer's pathology with accuracy close to a spinal tap or PET scan. The US FDA cleared the first blood test in May 2025 and a single-marker p-tau217 test in August 2026.
  • The first disease-modifying drugs. Antibodies that clear amyloid (lecanemab, donanemab) modestly slow Alzheimer's. An antisense drug, tofersen, treats the SOD1 form of ALS.
  • Genetic medicines. Antisense oligonucleotides, RNA interference and gene therapy can now lower a toxic protein at its source. A gene therapy for Huntington's (AMT-130) was submitted to the FDA in September 2026.
  • New ways to see. Cryo-electron microscopy revealed the atomic structure of the clumps in each disease. Single-cell sequencing shows which cell types change. Human stem cells let researchers grow a patient's own neurons in a dish.
Predict

Across Alzheimer's, Parkinson's and most forms of ALS, which factor raises risk the most?

Age dominates. Alzheimer's prevalence roughly doubles every five years after 65. Most cases of all three diseases are sporadic, meaning no single inherited mutation explains them. Genes, head injury and lifestyle shift risk, but none comes close to ageing. Chapter 4 builds a toy model of why ageing matters so much.

From gene to protein

Every disease in this course starts with a protein that behaves badly. To follow the arguments about why, you need to know how a gene becomes a protein, how mutations are written, and the difference between a protein that is missing and a protein that is toxic.

After this chapter you can
  • Read mutation names such as SOD1 A4V or LRRK2 G2019S.
  • Tell loss-of-function from gain-of-function, and say why the difference decides the therapy.
  • Explain repeat-expansion diseases, including why Huntington's onset depends on repeat length.

The central dogma, in engineering terms

DNA is long-term storage. Your genome holds about 3.1 billion base pairs written in four letters: A, C, G and T. Roughly 20,000 stretches of it are protein-coding genes, which together make up only about 1 to 2% of the genome. The rest includes switches that control when genes turn on, sequences that make functional RNAs, and much that is still poorly understood.

To use a gene, the cell copies it into RNA (transcription). Genes are interrupted: the parts that end up in the final message are exons, and the parts cut out are introns. Cutting and joining is called splicing, and it matters a great deal here: TDP-43, the protein that clumps in ALS, is a splicing regulator, and one approved drug class works by changing splicing.

The finished messenger RNA (mRNA) goes to a ribosome, which reads it three letters at a time. Each triplet, a codon, specifies one of 20 amino acids, or "stop". The chain of amino acids then folds into a working protein (translation). A typical protein is a few hundred amino acids long.

How mutations are written

Amino acids have one-letter codes. Biologists name a protein change by the original amino acid, its position, and the replacement:

  • SOD1 A4V: in the SOD1 protein, alanine (A) at position 4 is replaced by valine (V). It is the most common SOD1 mutation in North America and causes a fast form of ALS.
  • LRRK2 G2019S: glycine at position 2019 becomes serine. It is the most common single genetic cause of Parkinson's.
  • SNCA A53T: the first Parkinson's gene mutation ever found (1997), in α-synuclein.
  • APOE ε4: an older naming style. APOE comes in three common versions that differ at positions 112 and 158. ε2 has cysteine at both, ε3 has cysteine then arginine, ε4 has arginine at both.
  • A star or "X" means a stop codon, so Q12* would truncate the protein at position 12. DNA-level changes are written with "c." (for example c.1226A>G) and protein-level ones with "p.".

The ribosome reads in fixed triplets with no punctuation, so one inserted letter shifts every later codon. The downstream sequence becomes nonsense, and because 3 of the 64 codons are stops, a stop usually turns up within a few dozen codons. Cells also destroy many such mRNAs early through nonsense-mediated decay. Frameshifts and nonsense mutations therefore usually mean no protein from that copy of the gene.

Missing protein or toxic protein?

This is the most important question to ask about any disease gene, because the answer points to opposite treatments.

  • Loss of function: the mutation removes or breaks the protein. The fix is to restore it, for example by delivering a working gene. Recessive early-onset Parkinson's caused by PRKN or PINK1 works this way. So does frontotemporal dementia caused by GRN mutations, where losing one of two copies halves progranulin levels and that alone causes disease (haploinsufficiency).
  • Gain of function: the mutant protein does something harmful. The fix is to remove it. The cleanest example is SOD1 in ALS. Mice with the SOD1 gene deleted do not get ALS, but mice carrying human mutant SOD1 do. So the mutant protein is toxic, and lowering SOD1 should help. That reasoning produced tofersen, an antisense drug that lowers SOD1 and was approved in 2023.
  • Both at once: huntingtin is needed for embryonic development (mice without it die before birth), yet the expanded form is toxic. That is why there is a long debate about whether Huntington's drugs should lower only the mutant copy (allele-selective) or both copies.

Whether normal huntingtin is still needed in adult neurons, and how much. Useful evidence: adult mice in which huntingtin is removed after development, people who carry one inactivated copy of HTT (they appear largely healthy), and dose-response data from lowering trials. You would also want a biomarker showing that lowering actually happened in the brain, such as mutant huntingtin in spinal fluid, plus neurofilament light as an injury signal. Roche's tominersen trial (GENERATION HD1) was halted in 2021 after the high-dose groups did worse than placebo, which made this question concrete.

Repeat expansions

A distinctive class of mutation in neurology is the repeat expansion: a short sequence that normally repeats a few times gets copied too many times. Repeats are unstable, so they can lengthen further when passed to children and even inside a person's own cells over a lifetime.

Go deeper · the repeat keeps growing in the brain

Large genetic studies of people with Huntington's (the GeM-HD consortium) asked which other genes shift onset age. The strongest hits were DNA mismatch-repair genes such as MSH3, FAN1, PMS1 and MLH1. These genes control how much the CAG repeat expands somatically, in the person's own brain cells, over their lifetime.

A 2025 study in Cell (Handsaker and colleagues, McCarroll lab) measured repeat length in single striatal neurons from post-mortem brains. Repeats grew slowly for decades, then quickly once past about 80 CAGs. Neurons only showed widespread changes in gene activity, and then died, once their repeats became very long, beyond about 150. If that holds up, the inherited repeat is a clock, and slowing somatic expansion is a therapeutic target in its own right. Several companies now target MSH3 and related genes.

Another detail: the CAG tract in most people ends with a CAA codon (which also encodes glutamine). People who lack that interruption get sick earlier than their CAG count predicts. So the uninterrupted DNA length matters more than the number of glutamines.

Apply

Deleting the GRN gene in one copy causes frontotemporal dementia. A biotech proposes an antibody that blocks sortilin, a receptor that pulls progranulin into cells for destruction. What is the logic?

Right logic, and it is a real strategy: anti-sortilin antibodies raise progranulin in blood and spinal fluid. Other groups deliver an extra GRN gene with a virus. Both follow from the same diagnosis: too little protein. Whether raising a biomarker translates into clinical benefit is a separate question, and Chapter 14 is about how hard that is to show.
Recap
  • Gene → RNA (spliced) → protein. Codons are triplets; one inserted letter scrambles everything after it.
  • Mutation names read as original amino acid, position, new amino acid: G2019S.
  • Loss of function calls for adding protein back. Toxic gain of function calls for lowering it. Find out which one before choosing a therapy.
  • Repeat expansions are unstable, grow in children and in neurons, and onset tracks repeat length.

Folding and misfolding

A protein is useful only in the right shape. The proteins at the centre of neurodegeneration can take a second, wrong shape that copies itself onto normal molecules. This chapter explains that shape, the kinetics of how it spreads, and why those kinetics turned into a diagnostic test.

  • Explain "prion-like spread", "strains", and how a seed amplification assay works.

Normal folding

A newly made amino acid chain collapses so that its water-avoiding (hydrophobic) side chains end up inside, away from water. The final folded state is usually the lowest-energy arrangement the chain can find. Folding goes wrong often enough that cells keep helper proteins called chaperones (Hsp70 and Hsp90 are the famous families) to catch partly folded chains and give them another try.

Several proteins in this course are intrinsically disordered: in their normal state they have no fixed fold. Tau, α-synuclein and amyloid-β are disordered, and TDP-43 and FUS have long disordered tails. That flexibility suits their jobs, but it also lets them stack into a very stable wrong structure.

The amyloid fold

When these proteins misfold, they form amyloid: long, unbranched fibres about 10 nanometres wide. Inside each fibre, short stretches of protein called β-strands line up like the rungs of a ladder, about 4.7 ångströms (0.47 nm) apart, running perpendicular to the fibre's long axis. Each new molecule that joins adopts the same shape as the end it attaches to. This is called templating. It is what makes amyloid self-copying.

Amyloid is extremely stable, resists protein-cutting enzymes, and binds dyes such as Thioflavin T and Congo red. Thioflavin T fluoresces only when bound to amyloid, which makes it the standard way to measure aggregation in a test tube.

Vocabulary trap. "Amyloid" is a structure. "Amyloid-β" (Aβ) is one particular peptide that forms it. Tau, α-synuclein, TDP-43 and dozens of non-brain proteins can also form amyloid. When people say "the amyloid hypothesis" they mean amyloid-β. When a structural biologist says "an amyloid fibril of tau" they mean the fold.

The cast of proteins

Alzheimer's
Amyloid-β (Aβ)

A 38 to 43 amino acid fragment cut out of a larger membrane protein, APP, by two enzymes: β-secretase (BACE1) and γ-secretase, whose cutting core is presenilin (PSEN1, PSEN2). Aβ42 aggregates much faster than the more abundant Aβ40. Inherited Alzheimer's mutations raise the Aβ42-to-Aβ40 ratio. Forms plaques outside cells.

Alzheimer's, FTD, PSP
Tau

Normally binds and stabilises microtubules, the rails inside axons. Encoded by MAPT; made in six versions, grouped as 3R or 4R by how many repeat segments they contain. In disease it gets heavily phosphorylated, lets go of microtubules, and forms tangles inside neurons. The antibody AT8 (detects phosphorylation at S202/T205) is the standard stain.

Parkinson's, DLB, MSA
α-synuclein

A 140 amino acid protein concentrated at presynaptic terminals, where it helps manage neurotransmitter vesicles. Aggregates into Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites. Phosphorylation at serine 129 (pS129) marks the disease form.

ALS, FTD, LATE
TDP-43

An RNA-binding protein that normally lives in the nucleus and regulates splicing. In disease it leaves the nucleus, gets phosphorylated, and aggregates in the cytoplasm. The cell loses its nuclear job and gains a clump. Found in about 97% of ALS and about half of FTD.

Huntington's
Huntingtin

A very large protein (over 3,100 amino acids). The expanded polyglutamine stretch near its start makes fragments of it aggregate into inclusions, mostly in the nucleus.

Prion diseases
Prion protein (PrP)

The original self-copying misfolded protein. The misfolded form (PrPSc) is infectious in the strict sense: it can transmit disease between individuals, as in kuru, variant CJD and mad cow disease.

From one molecule to a deposit

Aggregation passes through a series of species. Single molecules (monomers) come together into small, unstable oligomers. Some oligomers convert into the ordered amyloid structure and grow into protofibrils and then fibrils, which bundle into the large deposits pathologists see: plaques, tangles, Lewy bodies.

Which species does the damage is one of the field's longest-running arguments. Much evidence points at small soluble oligomers, which can punch holes in membranes and disrupt synapses. Large inclusions may be partly a way for the cell to sequester the dangerous material. The answer matters for drug design: lecanemab was selected for binding soluble protofibrils, while donanemab binds a modified form of Aβ found in established plaques.

The kinetics: why there is a long quiet period

Forming the first stable nucleus is hard. A few molecules have to come together in exactly the right shape, which is rare. Once a fibril exists, adding molecules to its ends (elongation) is easy. And for Aβ42 and several other proteins, the side surface of existing fibrils catalyses brand new nuclei, a process called secondary nucleation. That creates a positive feedback loop. The result is a sigmoid curve: a long lag phase, explosive growth, then a plateau when the free protein runs out.

Halve the starting concentration. Does the half-time roughly double, or change more than that?

More than double. With secondary nucleation, half-time scales roughly as concentration to the power −1.5 in this model, so halving concentration multiplies the lag by roughly 2.6 here. That steep dependence is why gene dosage matters. People who carry a triplication of the α-synuclein gene (four copies in total) typically get Parkinson's in their 30s or 40s, earlier than people with a duplication (three copies), who in turn get it earlier than the general population. Researchers measure these scaling exponents to work out which microscopic step dominates, and which step a drug candidate blocks.

Spread between cells

If a seed can convert normal protein in a test tube, can it do so in a brain? Since about 2008 the answer has looked like yes. Fetal neurons grafted into Parkinson's patients' brains developed Lewy bodies over a decade, as if the disease had moved into them. Injecting synthetic α-synuclein fibrils into normal mouse brains triggers Lewy-like pathology that spreads along connected circuits (Luk and colleagues, 2012). Tau seeds behave similarly. This is called prion-like spread.

"Prion-like" describes cell-to-cell templating within one person. Alzheimer's and Parkinson's do not spread between people through ordinary contact. The one documented exception supports the mechanism: in 2024, researchers described Alzheimer's-type disease in several people who had received growth hormone extracted from cadaver pituitary glands as children, decades earlier.

Strains: the same protein, different folds

Prions taught researchers that one protein sequence can fold into several stable amyloid shapes, each copying itself faithfully and each causing a different disease. Cryo-EM confirmed this for human brain disease. Tau filaments from Alzheimer's, Pick's disease, chronic traumatic encephalopathy, corticobasal degeneration and progressive supranuclear palsy each have a distinct fold, consistent across patients with the same disease (Shi and colleagues, Nature 2021). α-synuclein from multiple system atrophy differs from α-synuclein in Parkinson's and Lewy body dementia. Disease identity seems to be written partly into the fold.

Go deeper · liquid droplets that turn solid

TDP-43 and FUS have low-complexity disordered regions that let them separate from the surrounding fluid into droplets, like oil in vinegar. This liquid–liquid phase separation is normal: it builds structures without membranes, such as stress granules, which form when a cell is under stress and dissolve afterwards.

Many ALS mutations in FUS and TDP-43 sit in exactly those regions, and in a test tube mutant droplets "age" faster into gels and solid fibres. One hypothesis is that repeated stress, over decades, eventually leaves some droplets unable to dissolve, and those become the seeds of pathology. It remains a hypothesis, and how well test-tube droplets reflect what happens in neurons is debated.

Explain

Antibodies against α-synuclein and tau were developed partly on the prion-like model. If spread between cells is the target, where does the antibody need to be, and what would that predict about its limits?

Show a model answer

Antibodies mostly stay outside cells, so they can only catch seeds in the brief period when seeds travel through the space between neurons. They cannot reach aggregates already inside cells, and they cannot stop a cell from making its own new aggregates. So such an antibody might slow spread to new regions but would do little for regions already affected. Expect modest effects, larger effects when started early, and weak effects on biomarkers of pathology already present. Several anti-tau antibodies that bind the N-terminal end of tau failed in trials, and one argument is that they bound the wrong part of tau to block seeding. Roche's anti-α-synuclein antibody prasinezumab missed its primary endpoint in phase 2 but showed signals that led to an ongoing phase 3 trial, due to complete in 2029.

Recap
  • Amyloid is a self-templating cross-β fold. Many proteins can form it; Aβ is just one.
  • Aggregation has a long lag set by slow nucleation, then fast growth. Seeds skip the lag; secondary nucleation makes it explosive.
  • Lag time depends steeply on concentration, which is why extra gene copies cause earlier disease.
  • Seeds can spread along connected neurons. Different folds (strains) track different diseases.

The cleanup crews

Every cell makes misfolded protein every day. Health depends on clearing it as fast as it appears. Neurons have to keep that balance for a lifetime, without the option of dividing to dilute damage. Many disease genes turn out to be parts of the cleanup machinery.

After this chapter you can
  • Describe the proteasome and autophagy–lysosome pathways and what each can handle.
  • Explain why PINK1, Parkin, GBA1 and LRRK2 are all called "lysosomal" or "quality-control" genes.
  • Use a toy model to explain why age is the dominant risk factor, and why treating early matters.

Biologists call the whole system that makes, folds and disposes of proteins proteostasis. It has three layers: chaperones that help folding, the proteasome for disposing of individual proteins, and the autophagy–lysosome system for bulk and large cargo.

The proteasome: a shredder for single proteins

Proteins marked for destruction get tagged with chains of a small protein called ubiquitin. A cascade of enzymes attaches the tag, and the last step is done by one of several hundred E3 ligases, which choose the targets. Parkin, encoded by a Parkinson's gene, is an E3 ligase. The tagged protein is threaded through the barrel-shaped proteasome, which unfolds it and chops it into short pieces.

The proteasome's entry channel is narrow, about 13 ångströms. It needs proteins to be unfolded first, so it cannot digest an amyloid fibril. Aggregates can even jam it.

Autophagy and the lysosome: bulk disposal

Autophagy ("self-eating") handles what the proteasome can't: aggregates, damaged organelles, invading bacteria. A double membrane wraps the cargo to form an autophagosome, which fuses with a lysosome. The lysosome is an acidic compartment (pH about 4.5 to 5) packed with around 60 digestive enzymes. Proteins with the right short signal sequence can also be delivered directly through a lysosomal receptor called LAMP2A; α-synuclein is one of them.

In neurons this system has a geography problem. Autophagosomes often form in distant axon terminals and have to be carried back to the cell body, where most mature lysosomes are, by the transport system described in the next chapter.

Mitochondria get recycled too

Damaged mitochondria leak electrons and make reactive oxygen species. Cells remove them by mitophagy. When a mitochondrion loses its membrane voltage, the kinase PINK1 builds up on its surface and adds phosphate to ubiquitin. That recruits and activates Parkin, which coats the mitochondrion with more ubiquitin, flagging it for autophagy. Losing both copies of PINK1 or PRKN causes Parkinson's that typically starts before age 40. It is some of the clearest evidence that failed quality control can kill dopamine neurons.

The lysosome as a Parkinson's hub

  • GBA1 encodes glucocerebrosidase, a lysosomal enzyme. Two broken copies cause Gaucher disease, a childhood storage disorder. One variant copy raises Parkinson's risk several-fold, more for severe variants. Depending on the population, GBA1 variants turn up in roughly 5 to 15% of people with Parkinson's, which makes them the most common genetic risk factor.
  • LRRK2 is a kinase, an enzyme that attaches phosphate groups to other proteins. Disease mutations such as G2019S make it overactive. Its best-known targets are Rab proteins (Rab10 and others) that direct membrane traffic, especially around lysosomes.
  • TMEM175, a lysosomal ion channel, is among the risk genes found in large genetic studies.
  • Many inherited childhood lysosomal storage diseases cause neurodegeneration. That pattern is consistent: neurons are unusually dependent on working lysosomes.
Check

A drug strongly activates the proteasome in neurons. Which kind of material is it least likely to help clear?

Fibrils can't be unfolded and threaded through the proteasome's narrow channel. They need autophagy and the lysosome. A proteasome activator could still help by catching misfolded monomers before they join aggregates, which is another way of saying it would work best early.

Why age matters so much

Clearance capacity falls with age. "Loss of proteostasis" and "disabled macroautophagy" are both on the standard list of hallmarks of ageing. Meanwhile a neuron cannot divide and hand half its junk to a daughter cell. Put a slowly falling capacity against a roughly constant load and you get a crossing point. Past that point, misfolded protein accumulates, and because aggregates template more aggregates, accumulation speeds up on its own.

Go deeper · what the toy model leaves out

It treats a whole brain as one compartment with one protein. Real neurons differ in load and capacity, aggregates spread between cells, inflammation and blood vessels add their own damage, and symptoms depend on which circuit loses how many neurons. Penetrance in real carriers is far below 100%: most people with a GBA1 variant never develop Parkinson's. The model shows a mechanism that could produce the age pattern. It is not evidence that the mechanism is right.

The neuron

A motor neuron's cell body sits in your spinal cord, and its axon reaches your toe, about a metre away. Supplying the far end of a cell that long is a logistics problem, and logistics failures show up again and again in neurodegeneration.

After this chapter you can
  • Name the parts of a neuron and the main neurotransmitters, and say which drugs act on which.
  • Explain axonal transport and estimate how long a delivery takes.
  • List the features that make some neurons vulnerable, and give one failed trial built on that idea.

Anatomy

  • Dendrites branch from the cell body and receive inputs from thousands of other neurons.
  • The cell body (soma) holds the nucleus and makes most of the cell's proteins.
  • The axon carries the output signal. It starts at the axon initial segment, where signals are triggered, and can run from under a millimetre to over a metre. Many axons are wrapped in myelin, an insulating sheath made by other cells, which speeds conduction.
  • The synapse is the junction. The presynaptic terminal stores neurotransmitter in small vesicles and releases it across a gap about 20 nanometres wide onto receptors on the next cell.

Electricity and its cost

A resting neuron holds its inside at about −70 millivolts relative to the outside, by pumping sodium out and potassium in with the sodium–potassium pump. An action potential is a roughly one-millisecond spike in which sodium channels open, the voltage flips positive, and the change travels down the axon at up to about 100 metres per second in large myelinated fibres. Resetting the ion gradients afterwards is the largest single use of the brain's energy. Neurons cannot pause this work, so anything that cuts their energy supply hurts them fast.

The supply chain: axonal transport

Most proteins are made in the cell body, yet most of the neuron's volume can be in the axon. Supplies travel on microtubules, polarised protein rails. Motor proteins walk along them: kinesins carry cargo outward (anterograde) and dynein carries cargo back (retrograde), including autophagosomes heading for lysosomes and survival signals from the target tissue.

Transport comes in speeds. Membrane vesicles move by fast transport, up to about 200 to 400 mm per day. Many soluble proteins, including α-synuclein and tau, move in slow component b at roughly 2 to 8 mm per day. The cytoskeleton itself, neurofilaments and tubulin, moves in slow component a at about 0.2 to 1 mm per day. Some of this is offset by local protein synthesis in axons, but most delivery still depends on the rails.

Mitochondria: the power plants on the move

Mitochondria make ATP and buffer calcium, and they have to be shipped to terminals and synapses, then shipped back for disposal. In 1982 several young people in California developed sudden, severe parkinsonism after injecting a contaminated synthetic opioid. The contaminant, MPTP, is converted in the brain to a poison of mitochondrial complex I that dopamine neurons take up selectively. The episode created the first good animal model of Parkinson's and pointed a lasting spotlight at mitochondria. Some pesticides, such as rotenone, inhibit the same complex.

What makes a neuron fragile

Vulnerable populations tend to share several features:

  • Huge axons. A single human dopamine neuron in the substantia nigra is estimated to have about 4.5 metres of finely branched, unmyelinated axon and one to two million synapses in the striatum. That is a vast area to supply and keep healthy.
  • Constant firing with calcium entry. Those same dopamine neurons fire on their own, like pacemakers, and part of the current comes through Cav1.3 calcium channels. Handling the calcium costs energy and stresses mitochondria. Dopamine itself can oxidise into reactive compounds.
  • Size and weak calcium buffering. Large motor neurons have relatively little of the calcium-binding proteins that protect other cells. The motor neurons that control eye movements, which ALS largely spares, have much more.
A hypothesis that went to trial. If calcium entry through Cav1.3 channels stresses nigral neurons, a blood-pressure drug that blocks those channels, isradipine, might slow Parkinson's. People who took similar drugs seemed to have lower Parkinson's risk in observational data. The phase 3 trial STEADY-PD III (336 patients, 36 months) found no benefit. Critics pointed out that the tolerable dose may have been too low to block the channels in the brain. You will see this pattern again: a plausible mechanism, supportive epidemiology, and a trial that can't tell whether the idea was wrong or the dose was.
Explain

Neurofilament light chain (NfL) is a cytoskeleton protein found almost only in neurons, mostly in large axons. Why would its level in blood make a good general marker of neurodegeneration, and a poor marker of which disease someone has?

Show a model answer

When axons break down, their contents leak into spinal fluid and then blood, so more axonal damage means more NfL, whatever the cause. It rises with ALS (very high, since large motor axons are lost fast), Huntington's, FTD, Alzheimer's, multiple sclerosis, stroke and head injury. That makes it a useful speedometer of damage and a good way to check whether a drug reduces damage. Tofersen's accelerated approval in 2023 rested largely on its drop in NfL. But because so many conditions raise it, NfL can't tell you which disease is present. It also rises gradually with normal age, so cut-offs must be age-adjusted.

Recap
  • Neurons are electrically busy, energy-hungry, and mostly irreplaceable.
  • Supplies ride microtubules; slow cargo to a one-metre axon can take years. Long axons suffer first when transport falters.
  • Vulnerable neurons combine large arbors, high activity with calcium load, and weak buffering.

Glia, barriers, and fluids

Neurons are about half the cells in the brain. The other half, the glia, were long treated as packing material. Human genetics changed that view: many Alzheimer's risk genes are switched on mainly in microglia. And every drug has to get past the barrier that glia help build.

Astrocytes: the support staff

Astrocytes are star-shaped cells that touch both synapses and blood vessels. They mop up released glutamate (through the transporter EAAT2, which is reduced in ALS spinal cord), buffer potassium, supply metabolic fuel, and help form and prune synapses. In disease they become "reactive": they swell, change thousands of genes, and release more of the protein GFAP. Plasma GFAP rises early in Alzheimer's and is now used as a biomarker. A 2017 study described toxic reactive astrocytes induced by microglia that kill neurons in culture. Later work showed reactive states are more varied than a simple good/bad split.

Microglia: the resident immune cells

Microglia are the brain's macrophages. They arrive from the embryonic yolk sac before birth and then renew themselves for life, separate from the blood's immune cells. Their processes constantly sweep the tissue. They eat debris, dead cells and excess synapses, which they find partly through "eat me" tags made of complement proteins (C1q, C3).

Genetics moved microglia to the centre of Alzheimer's research. In 2013, two groups found that a variant in TREM2, a receptor on microglia, raises Alzheimer's risk about two to four times. Since then, large genome-wide studies have found dozens of risk genes, and a striking share are expressed mostly in microglia: TREM2, PLCG2, ABI3, CD33, INPP5D, SPI1, the MS4A cluster. Single-cell studies in mice found a "disease-associated microglia" state that clusters around plaques and depends on TREM2.

Whether microglia help or hurt is context-dependent. Around plaques they seem to wall off and compact amyloid, which is protective. They can also strip synapses and drive inflammation. Anti-amyloid antibodies work partly by flagging plaques for microglia to eat.

Oligodendrocytes: insulation and fuel

Oligodendrocytes wrap axons in myelin and feed them lactate. In multiple system atrophy, α-synuclein aggregates build up inside oligodendrocytes rather than neurons, which is one reason MSA is considered a distinct disease from Parkinson's.

Predict

Most Alzheimer's risk variants found by genome-wide studies have small effects and sit near immune genes. What does this suggest, and what doesn't it prove?

A genetic association tells you the gene's pathway is causally involved, since your genes were fixed before the disease started. It doesn't tell you the direction. TREM2 risk variants reduce TREM2 function, which suggests that a weak microglial response is harmful, so drug developers made antibodies to activate TREM2. The first of these to report phase 2 results, AL002, did not slow decline. Genetics gave a target; it could not tell how to hit it.

The blood–brain barrier

Capillaries in the brain are sealed. Their endothelial cells are joined by tight junctions, wrapped by pericytes and astrocyte end-feet, and fitted with pumps (such as P-glycoprotein) that throw many foreign molecules back into the blood. This blood–brain barrier protects the brain from toxins and swings in blood chemistry. It also keeps out most drugs.

  • Small, fat-soluble molecules (roughly under 400 to 500 daltons, without many hydrogen-bonding groups) can diffuse across.
  • Transporters carry nutrients: glucose, amino acids, iron. Drugs that resemble nutrients can hitch a ride.
  • Large molecules mostly don't cross. Only about 0.1% of an antibody in the blood reaches the brain, so antibody drugs are given at high intravenous doses.
  • Receptor-mediated transcytosis: a few receptors, notably the transferrin receptor that brings in iron, carry their cargo all the way across the cell. "Brain shuttle" antibodies bind this receptor to get carried in.

Spinal fluid

The brain and spinal cord float in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), about 150 mL of it, made at roughly 500 mL per day, so it turns over several times daily. CSF can be sampled by lumbar puncture, a needle between the lower vertebrae below where the spinal cord ends. That makes CSF the best window into brain chemistry, and also a delivery route: drugs injected there (intrathecal dosing) bypass the barrier.

A separate question is how waste leaves the brain tissue itself. A 2013 mouse study proposed a "glymphatic" system in which CSF flows along blood vessels and flushes the tissue, faster during sleep. The idea is popular and contested; a 2024 mouse study measured lower clearance during sleep.

When the barrier matters for side effects

Anti-amyloid antibodies cause ARIA, amyloid-related imaging abnormalities, seen on MRI as swelling (ARIA-E, for edema) or small bleeds (ARIA-H, for hemosiderin). The leading explanation is that amyloid also coats the walls of brain blood vessels (cerebral amyloid angiopathy), and when antibodies and microglia attack it there, the vessel wall leaks. Most ARIA causes no symptoms, but some cases are serious and a few have been fatal. In the pivotal trials ARIA-E occurred in about 13% of lecanemab and 24% of donanemab patients, against about 2% on placebo. Risk is highest in people with two copies of APOE ε4, who have more vascular amyloid. European regulators approved both drugs only for people without two ε4 copies.

  • Astrocytes support and regulate neurons; reactive astrocytes release GFAP.
  • Microglia sit at the centre of Alzheimer's genetics, though whether to boost or calm them is unresolved.
  • The barrier keeps out large molecules. Options: make the drug small, use a transporter or shuttle, or inject into CSF or tissue.
  • CSF is both the main biomarker window and a delivery route.

Brain geography and staging

Symptoms tell you which circuits have failed. Pathologists learned that each disease advances through the brain in a fairly regular order, which they codified as stages. Those stage numbers show up constantly in papers.

  • Read Braak and Thal stages and say what they imply clinically.

A quick tour

  • Cerebral cortex. The folded outer sheet. Frontal lobe: planning, behaviour, and at its back edge the primary motor cortex. Parietal: sensation and spatial awareness. Temporal: hearing, language, and on its inner surface the memory system. Occipital: vision.
  • Hippocampus and entorhinal cortex. Tucked inside the temporal lobe. They form new memories of events. The entorhinal cortex is the hippocampus's main gateway and one of the first places tau tangles appear.
  • Basal ganglia. Deep nuclei that select and initiate movements: the striatum (caudate and putamen), globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus, and the substantia nigra in the midbrain.
  • Brainstem. Midbrain, pons and medulla. Controls breathing, heart rate, sleep and arousal, and houses small nuclei that send modulating signals across the brain: the locus coeruleus (noradrenaline), the raphe (serotonin), and the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus nerve, which talks to the gut.
  • Cerebellum. Coordination and timing. The main target of the spinocerebellar ataxias.
  • Spinal cord. Lower motor neurons sit in its front (ventral) horn and send axons to muscles.

The basal ganglia: go and stop

Simplified, the striatum's output runs through two pathways. The direct pathway releases the brake on the thalamus and lets a chosen movement go. The indirect pathway strengthens the brake and suppresses competing movements. Dopamine from the substantia nigra pushes the balance toward "go": it excites direct-pathway neurons through D1 receptors and inhibits indirect-pathway neurons through D2 receptors.

In Parkinson's, dopamine falls, the brake wins, and movement becomes slow and small (bradykinesia). Motor symptoms appear only after a large share of the dopamine terminals in the striatum is already lost, often estimated at half or more. That reserve is one reason the disease is silent for so long. Deep brain stimulation works by disrupting abnormal activity in the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus internus.

Predict

In early Huntington's, the striatal neurons of the indirect pathway die first. What happens to movement?

With the brake weakened, unwanted movements break through: the flowing, dance-like movements called chorea. Later in Huntington's the direct-pathway neurons die too, and patients become stiff and slow. The drugs approved for Huntington's chorea (tetrabenazine, deutetrabenazine, valbenazine) reduce dopamine release, pushing back toward "stop".

The motor system and ALS

Upper motor neurons in the motor cortex send axons down the spinal cord. Lower motor neurons in the brainstem and spinal cord connect to muscles. ALS kills both. Upper-neuron loss causes stiffness and overactive reflexes. Lower-neuron loss causes weakness, wasting and visible muscle twitches (fasciculations). Seeing both together is the clinical signature of ALS. Some motor neurons are spared until late: those controlling eye movements, and Onuf's nucleus, which controls the bladder sphincter. Comparing spared and vulnerable motor neurons is a standard way to search for protective factors.

Stages, and why they follow wiring

In 1991 Heiko and Eva Braak described six stages of tau tangle spread in Alzheimer's: starting in the transentorhinal region (I–II), moving through limbic regions including the hippocampus (III–IV), then into the wider neocortex (V–VI). Amyloid plaques follow a different order, described by Dietmar Thal in five phases, starting in the neocortex. In 2003 Braak proposed six stages for Parkinson's: α-synuclein first in the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus and the olfactory bulb, then upward through the pons and midbrain, with the substantia nigra reached around stage 3, and the cortex last.

These sequences follow anatomical connections, which fits the idea of seeds spreading along axons. Tau tangles correlate with symptoms much better than amyloid plaques do. Someone at Braak stage III or IV may have mild memory problems; dementia usually means stage V or VI.

The Parkinson's sequence explains the early non-motor symptoms: loss of smell, constipation (gut and vagus), and REM sleep behaviour disorder, in which people act out dreams because the brainstem circuit that paralyses muscles during dreaming has failed. About three-quarters of people with isolated REM sleep behaviour disorder develop Parkinson's, Lewy body dementia or MSA within about 12 years, which makes them a key population for prevention trials.

Go deeper · body-first or brain-first

The Danish neurologist Per Borghammer has proposed that Parkinson's has two main routes. In "body-first" disease, α-synuclein pathology starts in the gut's nervous system and climbs the vagus nerve to the brainstem; these people often have REM sleep behaviour disorder and constipation years before motor signs, and more symmetric disease. In "brain-first" disease, pathology starts in the brain, perhaps the olfactory system or amygdala, and the heart and gut are affected later. Imaging of heart nerves and gut tissue supports two patterns. People who had the vagus nerve cut decades ago may have slightly lower Parkinson's risk in some studies, though results are inconsistent.

Recap
  • AD: entorhinal cortex and hippocampus first. PD: brainstem and nigra. ALS: both motor neuron levels. bvFTD: frontal and anterior temporal lobes. HD: striatum.
  • Dopamine favours "go"; losing it slows movement. Losing indirect-pathway neurons releases unwanted movement.
  • Braak (tau, α-syn) and Thal (amyloid) stages describe ordered spread along connections; tau stage tracks symptoms best.

The genetics of risk

Human genetics is the field's most reliable source of causal evidence. Your genes were fixed before the disease began, so when a variant changes risk, the gene's pathway is upstream of the disease. This chapter covers how to read genetic findings without over- or under-reading them.

After this chapter you can

  • Convert an odds ratio into an absolute risk and explain why the two feel so different.
  • Read a Manhattan plot and say why a peak is not the same as a gene.

Three kinds of genetic evidence

  1. Mendelian (monogenic) mutations. Rare, with large effects. One mutation is enough to cause disease in most carriers. Examples: HTT expansions, APP/PSEN1/PSEN2 in early-onset Alzheimer's, SOD1 and C9orf72 in ALS. They give the clearest view of mechanism.
  2. Risk variants of moderate effect. Reasonably common, multiply risk two to fifteen times, and many carriers never get sick. Examples: APOE ε4, GBA1 variants, TREM2 R47H, LRRK2 G2019S (often called monogenic, but with incomplete penetrance).
  3. Common variants of small effect. Found by genome-wide association studies. Each nudges risk by 5 to 20%. Individually trivial, collectively informative about which pathways matter.

Penetrance and inheritance

Penetrance is the fraction of carriers who develop the disease, usually by a given age. Huntington's with 40 or more repeats is essentially fully penetrant. LRRK2 G2019S penetrance by age 80 is estimated at roughly a quarter to under a half, varying by study and population. C9orf72 expansions are highly penetrant but can show up as ALS, FTD, or both, sometimes in the same family.

Most of these genes are autosomal dominant: each child of a carrier has a 50% chance of inheriting the variant. Recessive genes such as PRKN and PINK1 cause disease only when both copies are broken. Some variants are common in particular populations because of founder effects: LRRK2 G2019S accounts for a large share of Parkinson's in Ashkenazi Jewish and North African Berber communities.

APOE, the biggest common risk gene

APOE makes apolipoprotein E, which carries cholesterol and other lipids between cells and is made in the brain mostly by astrocytes and microglia. Of the three common versions, ε3 is the most common, ε4 raises Alzheimer's risk and lowers age of onset, and ε2 lowers risk. About a quarter of people of European ancestry carry at least one ε4, and 2 to 3% carry two.

ε4 is associated with more and earlier amyloid, worse amyloid clearance, more vascular amyloid, and effects on microglia and lipid handling. In 2024, a large study (Fortea and colleagues, Nature Medicine) found that almost all ε4 homozygotes had Alzheimer's biomarker changes by age 65 and argued that two copies of ε4 should be considered a genetic form of the disease rather than a risk factor. Effect sizes differ by ancestry: ε4 raises risk less in people of African ancestry and more in people of East Asian ancestry.

Genome-wide association studies

A GWAS compares hundreds of thousands of people with and without a disease at millions of common DNA positions (single-nucleotide polymorphisms, or SNPs). Because it runs so many tests, a result only counts as "genome-wide significant" at p < 5 × 10⁻⁸. Results are drawn as a Manhattan plot: each dot is a SNP, placed along the chromosomes, with height equal to −log₁₀(p). True signals look like towers.

A tower is a region, not a gene. Nearby variants are inherited together (linkage disequilibrium), so many SNPs light up at once, and the gene name attached to a peak is often just the nearest one. Most hits are in non-coding DNA and probably change how much of a gene is made, in a particular cell type. Working out the actual gene and cell is called fine-mapping, and it is a field of its own. Recent studies report about 75 Alzheimer's loci and about 90 independent Parkinson's signals.

Go deeper · heritability, polygenic scores and the diversity gap

Heritability is the share of variation in a trait, in a population, explained by genetic variation. Twin studies put Alzheimer's heritability at roughly 60 to 80%, ALS around 60%, and Parkinson's lower, around 30%. Heritability is not the chance that your disease was "caused by genes".

Polygenic risk scores add up thousands of small effects. In Alzheimer's they help stratify risk beyond APOE, but they predict poorly in people whose ancestry differs from the studies they were built on.

Diversity: most large neurodegeneration GWAS were done in people of European ancestry. Projects such as the Global Parkinson's Genetics Program aim to fix this, and studies in African and Latin American populations have already found new risk variants, such as a GBA1 variant that is common in people of African ancestry and rare elsewhere.

Natural experiments: some families are famous because of what they teach. A Colombian extended family carries PSEN1 E280A and develops Alzheimer's in their 40s. One member who stayed well into her 70s carried two copies of a rare APOE variant (Christchurch), which pointed to APOE as a possible protective target. In Iceland, the APP A673T variant lowers Aβ production slightly and protects against Alzheimer's and age-related cognitive decline, which is some of the strongest human evidence that less Aβ is good.

Environment and modifiable risk

  • Parkinson's risk rises with exposure to certain pesticides (paraquat, rotenone) and the solvent trichloroethylene. Smoking and coffee drinking are associated with lower risk, a finding replicated many times and still not well explained.
  • ALS risk is associated with military service and possibly with intense physical activity and some environmental toxins; the evidence is weaker and mixed.
  • Dementia: the 2024 Lancet Commission listed 14 modifiable risk factors, including hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, physical inactivity, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, air pollution and social isolation, and estimated that nearly half of dementia cases could in principle be prevented or delayed. Estimates like this come from observational data, so they carry uncertainty about causation.
Recap
  • Genetic associations are causal anchors because genes come first; they still don't tell you the direction of an effect or the best drug.
  • Penetrance varies hugely. "Carries a risk gene" is not "will get the disease".
  • Odds ratios overstate risk when the outcome is common. Always ask for absolute numbers.
  • GWAS peaks are regions; finding the causal gene and cell takes more work.

Big hypotheses, and how to judge them

Every disease here has rival theories of what drives it. Experts don't settle these by authority; they weigh kinds of evidence that differ hugely in strength. Learning to rank evidence is the most useful skill in this course.

After this chapter you can
  • Rank kinds of evidence for a causal claim.
  • Lay out the case for and against the amyloid cascade hypothesis.
  • Recognise the "cause or consequence?" problem in any new finding.

The core difficulty

By the time anyone examines a patient's brain, decades of disease have happened. Almost everything is abnormal: proteins, inflammation, mitochondria, blood vessels, gene expression. Any of these might be a cause, a consequence, or a bystander. A finding of the form "X is higher in patients' brains" is the weakest kind of evidence, and it is also the most common kind of paper.

A ranking of evidence for human disease

  1. Human interventions. Changing X in randomised patients changes the disease. The strongest evidence, and the rarest.
  2. Human genetics. Variants that change X change disease risk, in the expected direction. Strong for causality because genes come first, though weaker on timing and size of effect.
  3. Timing in humans. In people followed over years, X changes before the disease does. Necessary for causation, not sufficient.
  4. Experiments in models. Adding X causes disease features in cells or animals; removing it prevents them. Strong within the model, uncertain in people.
  5. Association. X is different in patients. Hypothesis-generating only.

The best claims combine several levels that point the same way. A useful check: does a gene in the pathway change risk? If no human genetic evidence links a pathway to the disease, a drug targeting it starts with a handicap. Analyses of drug pipelines have estimated that targets with human genetic support are roughly twice as likely to succeed.

The amyloid cascade hypothesis

Proposed by John Hardy and Gerald Higgins in 1992, the hypothesis states that the build-up of amyloid-β is the initiating event in Alzheimer's. It triggers tau pathology, inflammation, synapse loss, neuron death and dementia, in that order. It has shaped thirty years of drug development and remains contested. The TDP-43 story, as a model of good reasoning

When TDP-43 leaves the nucleus, its normal job of blocking certain hidden pieces of introns from being spliced into mRNA fails. These cryptic exons then get included. In 2019, two groups found that TDP-43 loss inserts a cryptic exon into STMN2, a gene for axon repair, destroying its protein. In 2022, two more groups found the same in UNC13A, a gene for neurotransmitter release.

Here is why that was convincing. UNC13A was already one of the strongest hits in ALS and FTD genome-wide studies, and nobody knew why. It turned out the risk variants sit inside the cryptic exon and make it easier to include. So the genetic evidence (level 2) and the cell biology (level 4) lock together: losing TDP-43's splicing function contributes causally to disease, in humans. The cryptic exons are human-specific, which is also why mouse models had missed them.

Apply

A new paper reports that a mitochondrial stress marker is three times higher in post-mortem ALS spinal cord than in controls. What is the best next experiment for testing whether it is causal?

More post-mortem data and another model with the same association don't move the evidence up a level. Genetic evidence from humans and an intervention in a relevant human cell system do. Stating the next experiment this way is also a good way to sound like a colleague in a lab meeting.
Explain

Show a model answer

  • Amyloid-β is probably an initiator in Alzheimer's but not the whole story once tau spreads.
  • The strongest findings tie genetics and mechanism together, as with TDP-43 and UNC13A.

Biomarkers and diagnosis

For most of the twentieth century these diseases were confirmed only at autopsy. Biomarkers changed that: they let doctors see the biology in living people, years before symptoms, and let trials check whether a drug hit its target. They are also where an engineer's sense for measurement pays off most.

After this chapter you can
  • Name the main imaging and fluid biomarkers and what each measures.
  • Explain the order in which biomarkers turn abnormal in Alzheimer's.
  • Compute what a positive test result means from sensitivity, specificity and prevalence.

What a biomarker is for

A biomarker is any measurable signal of a biological state. The same measurement can serve different jobs, and papers should say which:

  • Diagnostic: is the disease biology present? (amyloid PET)
  • Prognostic: how fast will this person decline? (tau PET, NfL)
  • Pharmacodynamic or target engagement: did the drug do what it was meant to do in the body? (CSF SOD1 protein falling on tofersen; CSF phospho-Rab10 on a LRRK2 inhibitor)
  • Safety: is the drug causing harm? (MRI for ARIA)
  • Surrogate endpoint: a biomarker accepted in place of a clinical outcome. The FDA's accelerated approval pathway accepts surrogates "reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit". Amyloid reduction supported lecanemab's 2023 accelerated approval; NfL reduction supported tofersen's.

Imaging

  • MRI: structure. Hippocampal shrinkage in Alzheimer's, frontal and temporal shrinkage in FTD, and the safety scans for ARIA.
  • Amyloid PET: a radioactive tracer that binds plaques. Results are reported on the Centiloid scale, where 0 is the average of young amyloid-negative people and 100 the average of mild Alzheimer's dementia. Values above roughly 20 to 30 are usually read as positive. In the donanemab trial, dosing stopped once amyloid fell below 11 Centiloids on one scan, or below 25 on two scans in a row.
  • Tau PET: binds tau tangles and reproduces Braak-like stages in living people. It predicts decline better than amyloid PET.
  • FDG-PET: glucose use; shows which regions are failing.
  • DaT-SPECT: dopamine transporter density in the striatum; shows loss of dopamine terminals in Parkinson's and related disorders.

Fluids

  • CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio: falls when plaques form, because Aβ42 gets trapped in them.
  • Phosphorylated tau (p-tau181, p-tau217, p-tau231): rises early in Alzheimer's, tracking amyloid-driven changes in tau. Plasma p-tau217 is now the best blood marker, and FDA-cleared tests use it.
  • Neurofilament light: axon damage of any cause.
  • GFAP: astrocyte activation; rises early in Alzheimer's.
  • α-synuclein seed amplification assay: detects misfolded α-synuclein seeds in CSF (chapter 3). In the PPMI cohort it was positive in about 88% of people with Parkinson's and negative in about 96% of healthy controls, with lower sensitivity in LRRK2 carriers.
  • RT-QuIC: the same principle for prion disease.
  • Emerging: cryptic peptides from TDP-43-dependent cryptic exons, measured in CSF as a readout of TDP-43 loss; mutant huntingtin in CSF.

Blood tests became possible because assays got thousands of times more sensitive. Brain proteins in blood are at femtomolar levels. Technologies such as single-molecule arrays (Simoa) and targeted mass spectrometry can count them.

The order of events in Alzheimer's

The clearest timeline comes from families with dominantly inherited Alzheimer's (the DIAN study), where each person's expected age of onset can be estimated from their parent's. Bateman and colleagues (NEJM 2012) found that CSF Aβ42 starts falling about 25 years before expected symptom onset, amyloid builds up on PET and tau rises in CSF about 15 years before, brain shrinkage about 15 years before, glucose use and memory change about 10 years before, and global cognitive impairment about 5 years before.

From biology to definitions

In 2024 an Alzheimer's Association workgroup (Jack and colleagues) revised the criteria so that Alzheimer's disease is defined biologically: an abnormal "Core 1" biomarker (amyloid PET, a CSF amyloid ratio, or an accurate plasma p-tau217 test) is enough, even without symptoms. Biological stages A to D follow tau PET; clinical stages 0 to 6 follow symptoms. The International Working Group disagreed in print, arguing that a diagnosis should require symptoms, and that biomarker-positive healthy people should be called "at risk", since many will never decline in their lifetime. The disagreement matters for who gets enrolled in trials and, eventually, who gets treated.

Parkinson's is following the same path. The 2024 Neuronal α-Synuclein Disease Integrated Staging System (NSD-ISS) defines disease by an Synuclein seed test and Dopaminergic imaging, with stages 0 to 6. The competing SynNeurGe framework adds Neurodegeneration and Genetics as separate axes.

What does a positive result mean?

Sensitivity is the share of people with the disease who test positive. Specificity is the share of people without it who test negative. What a patient wants to know is different: given a positive result, how likely is it that I have the disease? That is the positive predictive value, and it depends heavily on how common the disease is in the group being tested.

Mice: indispensable and misleading

Most Alzheimer's mouse models overexpress human genes with familial mutations. The widely used 5xFAD mouse carries five such mutations and develops plaques by about two months of age, but little neuron loss and no tangles. Removing plaques in these mice is easy, which partly explains why so many anti-amyloid strategies looked good before failing in people. Newer knock-in models change the mouse's own gene instead of adding extra copies, avoiding overexpression artefacts.

ALS offers a cautionary tale. In 2008, the ALS Therapy Development Institute re-tested more than 70 compounds that had been reported to extend survival in SOD1-G93A mice, using large, sex-balanced, litter-matched groups. None worked. Most of the original effects were noise from small, poorly controlled studies, a problem now addressed by reporting guidelines such as ARRIVE.

Human stem cells

In 2006–2007, Shinya Yamanaka showed that adult skin or blood cells could be reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), which can become any cell type. Researchers can now take a patient's cells and grow their neurons, microglia or astrocytes. Forcing expression of one transcription factor, NGN2, turns iPSCs into cortical-like neurons in about two weeks, fast enough for large screens.

Two cautions. First, reprogramming erases the cell's age, so iPSC neurons resemble fetal neurons. Some labs convert skin cells directly into neurons, skipping the stem-cell stage, which preserves some age-related features. Second, different people's cell lines differ in many ways besides the mutation of interest. The standard fix is an isogenic control: use CRISPR to correct the mutation in the patient line (or introduce it into a healthy line), so the only difference is the variant.

Hybrid systems are growing: human microglia transplanted into mouse brains, for example, behave much more like human brain microglia than they do in a dish.

How antisense works

An antisense oligonucleotide is a short strand of modified nucleotides that pairs with one specific RNA. Two designs dominate. A gapmer has a central DNA-like section that, when bound, recruits the enzyme RNase H1 to destroy the RNA, so less protein is made. A steric blocker sits on a splice site and changes how the RNA is spliced, without destroying it. Nusinersen, approved for spinal muscular atrophy in 2016, is a steric blocker that makes cells include a missing exon. Its success, followed by tofersen, made antisense the leading platform for genetically defined neurological disease.

Antisense drugs don't cross the blood–brain barrier, so they're injected into spinal fluid. From there they reach the spinal cord and the outer layers of the brain well, and deep structures such as the striatum less well. That is one reason AMT-130 for Huntington's is injected directly into the striatum.

Three ideas that decide whether a drug can work

  • Target engagement: did the drug reach its target and change it, in the right tissue, by enough? The 2026 LUMA trial of the LRRK2 inhibitor BIIB122 is the textbook case. In blood, LRRK2 activity fell by over 90%. In spinal fluid, the downstream marker phospho-Rab10 fell by only about 30% at its peak. The trial showed no slowing of Parkinson's (hazard ratio 1.04). It cannot tell whether LRRK2 inhibition doesn't help, or whether the brain never got enough.
  • Therapeutic window: the gap between the dose that helps and the dose that harms. BACE1 inhibitors, which block the first cut that makes Aβ, slightly worsened cognition in several large trials, probably because BACE1 has other substrates needed at synapses.
  • Selectivity: can you hit the bad thing and spare the good? For dominant diseases, an allele-selective drug lowers only the mutant copy, usually by targeting a common DNA variant that happens to sit on the same chromosome as the mutation in that patient.

Clinical trials

A therapy is only real once a trial shows it helps people. Trials in neurodegeneration are long, large and noisy, and the statistics decide what counts as a result. You will read more trial results than any other kind of paper, so this chapter goes into the numbers.

After this chapter you can
  • Describe trial phases, endpoints and the main rating scales.
  • Explain power, and why small positive trials overestimate effects.
  • Judge "27% slowing" against what patients would notice.

Phases and approvals

  • Phase 1: safety and how the body handles the drug (pharmacokinetics). In gene-targeted neurology, often in patients, with biomarkers of target engagement.
  • Phase 2: dose finding and early signs of effect, usually on biomarkers and sometimes clinical measures. Typically tens to a few hundred people.
  • Phase 3: the pivotal test of benefit, usually hundreds to a few thousand people over 1.5 to 3 years.
  • Approval: traditional approval needs evidence of clinical benefit. Accelerated approval in the US accepts a surrogate endpoint reasonably likely to predict benefit, on condition that a confirmatory trial follows.

Design vocabulary

  • Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled: the gold standard. For surgery-based treatments the control may be a sham operation.
  • Primary endpoint: the one outcome declared in advance as the test. Everything else is secondary or exploratory and carries less weight.
  • Change from baseline, typically analysed with a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM), for rating-scale outcomes.
  • Time-to-event outcomes (time to death, to ventilation, to confirmed worsening) are summarised by a hazard ratio. HR 0.7 means the event rate on drug is 30% lower; HR 1.0 means no difference.
  • Open-label extension: after the blinded period, everyone gets the drug. In a delayed-start analysis, if the group that started early stays ahead of those who started late, that suggests the drug changed the disease course rather than just symptoms. Tofersen's case leans on this kind of evidence.
  • External control: treated patients compared with untreated people from a natural-history study instead of a randomised placebo group. Faster, and vulnerable to bias, because the groups were not selected or assessed the same way.

What "27% slowing" means

In the Clarity AD trial, people on placebo worsened by 1.66 CDR-SB points over 18 months; people on lecanemab worsened by 1.21. The difference, 0.45 points, is 27% of the placebo decline. Both numbers are true. Which one you hear depends on who is talking.

Is 0.45 points meaningful? Estimates of the minimal clinically important difference for CDR-SB are roughly 1 point in mild cognitive impairment and 1.5 to 2 in mild dementia. By that standard, the average effect is below what an individual would notice at 18 months. The counter-arguments: an MCID was designed for one person's change, not a group difference; the gap may widen with time (extension data suggest it continues); and 27% slower decline can be expressed as about five months of independence gained over 18 months. This argument is not settled, and it determines how health systems pay for these drugs.

Power and noise

A trial's job is to separate a drug effect from the variation between people. The power of a trial is the probability it detects a true effect of a given size. It depends on the effect size, on how variable the outcome is, and on the number of patients. Required sample size scales with variance divided by effect squared, so halving the effect needs four times the patients.

Why trials fail

  • Wrong target: the biology was not causal, or not in people.
  • Not enough target engagement in the brain (LUMA).
  • Too late: the process had become self-sustaining (early anti-amyloid trials in moderate dementia).
  • Wrong patients: before amyloid PET was required, a substantial share of participants in some Alzheimer's trials turned out to have no amyloid at all.
  • Noisy, slow endpoints that need huge trials to detect modest effects.
  • Strong placebo responses, especially in Parkinson's motor scores.

Newer designs

  • Platform trials test several drugs against a shared placebo group under one protocol. The HEALEY ALS Platform Trial has run multiple drugs this way; none met its primary endpoint so far, but each answer came faster and cheaper.
  • Prevention trials in genetic carriers treat people who will almost certainly develop disease before symptoms start, such as DIAN-TU (dominant Alzheimer's) and planned prion-disease trials.
  • Biomarker enrichment: requiring a positive amyloid PET, p-tau217, or seed assay at entry so everyone has the target biology.
  • External and synthetic controls from natural-history data, with statistical matching. The AMT-130 story in Huntington's shows the trade-off. The FDA first rejected the external comparison as a basis for approval, then in June 2026 accepted it for an accelerated-approval filing. At four years the comparison weakened (44% slowing, not statistically significant). The company argued that the sickest people drop out of the natural-history study over time, so the remaining controls look healthier and the drug's effect is understated. Sceptics read the same problem as a sign of how fragile external comparisons are.

A gene therapy is injected into the brain during a long MRI-guided operation. Why is a randomised placebo trial hard here, and what would you accept instead?

Show a model answer

A true placebo needs sham surgery: anaesthesia, a skull opening and partial needle passes, with real risks and no possible benefit. It is ethically difficult, especially in a fatal disease, and recruitment suffers. Alternatives: a delayed-treatment control (sham now, real treatment later); a randomised comparison of doses, which shows a dose–response; or an external control from a large, well-characterised natural-history cohort, with pre-specified matching and outcomes measured the same way. Objective biomarkers (target lowering in CSF, MRI volume, NfL) reduce the reliance on rating scales that knowledge of treatment can bias. Each option trades certainty for feasibility, and reasonable experts disagree on where to draw the line.

Recap
  • Read the primary endpoint, the absolute difference, and the confidence interval before any percentage.
  • Small significant trials exaggerate effects. Expect phase 3 to shrink them.
  • Ask whether the control group was randomised; if external, ask how it was matched.

Reading a paper

Papers in this field are dense, full of abbreviations, and written for insiders. A reliable reading routine, and a short list of things to check, get you most of the way. The rest is practice.

After this chapter you can
  • Read a paper in three passes and pull out its claim, evidence and weak points.
  • Interpret error bars, survival curves, volcano plots and UMAPs.
  • Spot the most common problems in preclinical and clinical papers.

Know what you're reading

  • Primary research: new data. High-profile venues include Nature, Science, Cell, Neuron, Nature Neuroscience and Nature Medicine; disease-focused ones include Acta Neuropathologica, Brain, Annals of Neurology, Alzheimer's & Dementia and Movement Disorders. Trials appear in NEJM, The Lancet, Lancet Neurology, JAMA and JAMA Neurology.
  • Reviews: summaries of a topic. Start any new topic with a recent review in Nature Reviews Neurology, Neuron or an Annual Review.
  • Preprints on bioRxiv and medRxiv: not yet peer reviewed. Often where the newest work appears first.
  • Trial registrations on ClinicalTrials.gov: check that the primary endpoint reported is the one registered.
  • Conference presentations and press releases: "topline" results usually arrive by press release, then at a meeting (AAIC, AD/PD, CTAD, the MDS Congress, ENCALS), months before a paper. Press releases omit what the company would rather you didn't ask.

Three passes

  1. Ten minutes. Title, abstract, the last paragraph of the introduction (where the authors state what they did), figure titles, and the first paragraph of the discussion. Write the main claim in one sentence.
  2. One hour. Go figure by figure, reading the results text beside each. For every panel ask: what is measured, in what system, with what n, against what control? Most of a paper's argument lives in its figures.
  3. Deep. Methods and supplementary material. Could you reproduce it? What was not shown? This pass is for papers you will build on.

Figure literacy

  • Bar charts with error bars: find out whether the bars show SD (spread of the data), SEM (precision of the mean) or a 95% confidence interval. Look for individual data points.
  • Survival (Kaplan–Meier) curves: the share of each group still event-free over time. Ticks mark people who left the study (censored). Look at the hazard ratio and its confidence interval, and at how many people remain at the later time points, often printed under the axis.
  • Volcano plots: each dot is a gene or protein; horizontal position is log₂ fold change, vertical is −log₁₀ of the p value. Hits are top left and top right. Check that significance was corrected for testing thousands of genes (false discovery rate).
  • UMAP and t-SNE plots: single-cell data squashed into two dimensions. Clusters are meaningful; distances between clusters, and cluster sizes, mostly are not.
  • Forest plots: effect estimates with confidence intervals, often for subgroups. If an interval crosses 1 (for ratios) or 0 (for differences), that estimate alone is not significant. Subgroup effects are hypothesis-generating unless pre-specified.
  • "Representative images": a picture chosen by the authors. Trust the quantification across all samples, not the photo.

A checklist for preclinical papers

  • What is n, and what is the unit? Animals, independent differentiations, or patients, not cells or images.
  • Were animals randomised and outcomes scored blind? Were both sexes used?

  • Did the drug reach the target tissue at a relevant concentration?

  • Is the finding replicated in a second model, line or cohort?
  • Does the conclusion claim more than the data support?

Where engineers fit

You now know where the bottlenecks are. Several of them are measurement and automation problems more than biology problems. This chapter maps them to engineering work, and shows with one equation why better measurement can matter as much as a better drug.

The equation that connects measurement to cures

From chapter 14: the number of patients a trial needs scales with the variance of the outcome divided by the square of the effect. The variance has two parts: real differences between patients' disease courses, and measurement noise. Clinic rating scales are measured a few times a year, by different raters, on a patient's good or bad day. If the noise part is large, averaging many cheap measurements (a sensor at home every day instead of a clinic exam every three months) can shrink the variance and the trial with it. Smaller, faster trials mean more drugs tested per decade.
